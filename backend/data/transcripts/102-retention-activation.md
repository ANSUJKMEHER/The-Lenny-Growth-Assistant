In this episode we dig into the metrics that actually move the needle for a growing
product, and why most dashboards are full of vanity numbers that tell you almost
nothing. If you only look at monthly active users and total signups, you are flying
blind.

Let us start with activation. Activation is the moment a new user first experiences
the core value of your product. It is not when they create an account, and it is not
when they log in for the first time. It is when they do the one thing that makes the
product worth paying for. For a note-taking app that might be creating their first
note and sharing it. For a hiring tool it might be posting a job and receiving the
first qualified candidate.

The reason activation matters so much is that it is the strongest predictor of
retention. Users who activate in their first session are dramatically more likely
to still be around ninety days later. Users who never activate almost always churn.
So the single highest-leverage thing you can do early on is shorten the time to value.

A common framework for thinking about this is the aha moment. Find the action that,
when a user performs it, correlates with long-term retention, then redesign your
onboarding to drive every new user to that action as fast as possible. Remove every
step that does not directly serve that goal.

Retention is the next piece. I like to look at retention in cohorts, not in a single
blended number. A blended number hides the fact that your newest cohort is behaving
very differently from your oldest cohort. Weekly cohort retention, bucketed by signup
week, tells you whether the product is actually getting better or worse over time.

There is a useful rule of thumb I will repeat here. For a consumer subscription
product, a healthy weekly retention curve might flatten out around twenty to thirty
percent after a couple of months. For a B2B tool where usage is part of a weekly
workflow, you want to see much higher, often above sixty percent. If your numbers are
far below those ranges, do not panic. The point is to establish your own baseline and
then improve it week over week.

Churn deserves special attention because it is the silent killer. Churn compounds.
If you lose five percent of customers every month, you are replacing more than half
your base every year. Reducing churn is usually cheaper and more impactful than
acquiring new customers, because you already paid to get those users in the door.

One of my favorite churn tactics is the exit survey. When a user cancels, ask a single
open-ended question about why. You will be surprised how consistent the answers are.
Usually it is one of three things: they never activated, the price was wrong, or they
found a substitute. Fix the first of those and you will move churn more than any
retention campaign.

So the summary is: track activation first, measure retention in cohorts, and treat
churn as a product bug rather than a marketing problem. Do that and your dashboards
will finally tell you the truth.
